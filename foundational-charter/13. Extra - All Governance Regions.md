# All Governance Regions

**Governance region** can act like a large umbrella, covering cities, towns, villages, boroughs, and local communities without getting trapped in one country’s exact administrative vocabulary.<br/>

Every governance region should have sufficient autonomy.<br/>

The government establishes an **Autonomy Department** to identify governance regions that have not held democratic elections or achieved self-governance. The department recruits officials and sends them, in groups of three, to live in these governance regions.<br/>

These officials will start as ordinary citizens to organize elections and manage these regions. Local citizens can participate in democratic elections and exercise their right to self-governance at any time.<br/>

Citizens may hold multiple identities at the same time, such as **Governance Region Supervisory Council**, **National Supervisory Council member**, government official, and others.<br/>

## Offcials - The Tri-Vector Commander

For larger governance regions, such as cities, the mayor may establish a hierarchical structure and dispatch officials to administer governance. Officials at each tier operate under the Tri-Vector Commander system.<br/> 

The three officials vote to elect the Executive Official, while the other two officials serve as commissioners. If the vote totals differ, the official with the most votes becomes the Executive Official. If the vote count is tied, the official ranked higher by the system will be elected as the Executive Official.<br/>

Each year, two new officials replace the two commissioners. The commissioners do not manage the prison, they only oversee it.<br/>

The three officials can vote at any time to elect a new Executive Official.<br/>

Three officials must evaluate the two officials other than themselves. Each evaluation result must be positive, negative, or withheld.<br/>

For example, A is the Executive Official, and B and C are commissioners.<br/>

A, B, and C each vote for themselves, and A retains the Executive Official position.<br/>

B votes for C. C becomes the new Executive Official, and A becomes a commissioner.<br/>

B votes for himself again, and C remains the Executive Official.<br/>

## Example - A governance area dominated by the wealthy

When the majority of citizens in a governed region are wealthy individuals and merchants, the effects of majority voting create a governance area tailored for the wealthy. The mayor serves the wealthy, imposing high fixed property taxes on the poor while retaining only the wealthy. Meanwhile, the mayor applies minimal property taxes to the vast lands of the rich, ensuring they enjoy the best lifestyle and high-quality services provided by the mayor and merchants.<br/>

## Example - A governance region dominated by average people

When the majority of citizens in a governed region are ordinary people, the effects of majority voting create a governance area belonging to the common people. The mayor serves ordinary people, imposing high fixed property taxes on the wealthy, which causes the rich to either leave or contribute a substantial amount of tax revenue. The mayor applies minimal fixed property taxes to the land of ordinary people—whether for living or running shops—ensuring they have the best possible living experience.<br/>
